It’s normal to wonder

Forgetting a name isn’t the same as forgetting who.

Most memory changes are part of aging. A few are early signs of something we can act on. The hard part is not knowing the difference — and that’s exactly what this is for.

Most of what you’re feeling is normal.

Walking into a room and forgetting why. Losing a word. Misplacing keys. These are usually attention or stress — not memory disease.

Some changes are early signs.

Repeating questions, getting lost on familiar routes, struggling with money or planning. These deserve a closer look — the earlier the better.

Knowing gives you options.

If something’s changing, catching it now — while there are still treatable causes and lifestyle changes that matter — gives you years you wouldn’t otherwise have.

Why early matters

The earlier you know, the more you can do.

If memory changes are early signs of mild cognitive impairment (MCI), there’s a window — often three years or more — where lifestyle, treatment of reversible causes, and supported planning can change the path you’re on.

That window narrows the longer you wait. Most people who later receive an Alzheimer’s diagnosis had years of subtle change before anyone screened them. You don’t have to wait that long.

Your questions, answered

The things people ask before they start.

Is this a diagnosis?
No. This is a screen, not a diagnosis. It tells you whether your memory looks within the normal range for someone your age — and if it doesn’t, what to do next. Only a clinician can give a diagnosis.
Is it really private?
Yes. You don’t need an account to take the screen. Your responses are stored under HIPAA-aligned privacy and are never sold to anyone. If you want, you can download your result and share it with your doctor — that’s your choice, not ours.
Will it tell me I have Alzheimer’s?
No screen can do that, including this one. What this can tell you is whether your performance looks like normal aging or something worth a closer look. If your result suggests the latter, we’ll point you toward the right next step.
How is this different from the test my doctor might give me?
Most quick cognitive screens used in doctor’s offices — like the MMSE — were designed to detect dementia. By the time they’re positive, the window for early action has already narrowed. The MCI Screen was built specifically to catch what comes before dementia: mild cognitive impairment.
I’m worried it’ll be too hard. Or too sad.
It’s gentler than people expect. The questions are everyday, the memory check is short, and the result is written in plain language — no clinical scores you have to decode alone. If you’d rather have someone with you, take it with a family member.
What happens after I see my result?
If your result is reassuring, you can simply put it away and check again in a year. If it suggests something to look into, you’ll see a clear next step — usually a conversation with your primary-care doctor, with a printable summary they can put straight into your chart.
I’m taking this for a parent. Is that okay?
Absolutely. Many people take it for a parent, partner, or sibling who isn’t ready to do it themselves. There’s a short version that lets you answer on their behalf — it’s called an informant screen, and it’s often the most accurate way for a worried family member to find out where things stand.
